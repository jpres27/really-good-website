How I learned how to program

by John Presley

2025-4-16

This was inspired by a post by Dan Luu which I found to be a rare example of genuine reflection. There is a great deal of writing concerning the bildung of the programmer or engineer, but I have found that most people try to pass off their experience as also directly containing useful advice for everyone that can be easily distilled. As Dan makes clear, this is not self-evidently true and people do not seem to think very much on this when offering advice. To make the matter even more uncertain, many people who write about programming as a career tend to find the locus of causality always squarely within their own actions and ambition. Dan mentions that while he let his friends read drafts of his essay, they felt he attributed too much too luck when in reality all of his opportunity came from his own hard work. While he admits that there is possibly some truth in that, he goes on to demonstrate how most of his great opportunities really came from decisions made based on bad information or bad heuristics.

I really didn't expect to find a programmer's description of their journey to begin with an account of how they nearly didn't graduate from high school but it immediately assured me that I was in good hands. Too many stories in this genre are about how the subject simply did well in high school and then did well in college and was highly motivated and knew what they wanted to do and then did very well in industry and now they are telling you how well they did and how you can be like them if you just do everything right. Their writing often feels as though its not addressed to anyone else, and this may be for the best.

It has become quite popular for people to tell themselves and others stories about their success, or their search for success and what kind of person they have become in this pursuit. They are very rarely reflective on the limits of their agency even when these are obvious facts of anyone's existence. It is good to focus your efforts on what is within your control, but delusional to then extend your model of the limits of your control beyond the proper scope. I think this is sufficient for preface, so we shall proceed to my own little tale.

The early years

I grew up on the personal computer. I somehow learned to use DOS before the age of 5 well enough to initiate a video game we had on our first family computer of my lifetime. I remember very little of this besides the moment when the commands I entered worked and the video mode changed as the game started to be drawn on screen. I have a clearer recollection of after we had switched to Windows 3.1. My dad must have purchased Myst and The Journeyman Project: Turbo! because both CD-ROMs were on the shelf and I played them fairly regularly. One of my parents would occasionally help me with some of the puzzles that I found difficult, which was most of the puzzles at that age. What I really got out of both of those games was the feeling of walking around in a truly strange, virtual world. The pre-rendered 3D backgrounds were unlike anything I had seen prior. I spent hours and hours just walking around and interacting with things without any particular goal of beating the games or winning anything. I just wanted to explore and experiment and interact.

I continued to have a very close relationship to the computer as I grew up, eventually getting involved in forums, IRC servers, making Super Mario inspired webcomics in MS Paint, and so on. A kid I met on my second or third day of middle school told me about Runescape so I went home and typed it into Ask Jeeves or Google, I can't remember which. At that time, Runescape was still 2D sprites and a pretty primitive 3D world, now called Runescape Classic. I made a character and walked around killing chickens and goblins and drinking the beer they dropped. Prior to this time I had played some real-time strategy games which my brother had brought home, like Command and Conquer: Red Alert 2 and Starcraft. And I had a handful of games on the Nintendo 64. But Runescape, with its sandbox design and relative freedom, gave me a glimpse of the aspects of gaming which I had been seeking when playing the old adventure puzzle games.

It also lead me to join a forum dedicated to free online games, among them Runescape. I would end up posting here for probably 7 or 8 years and meeting the closest friends I would have throughout middle school and some of high school. It was here that I met the first person who ever talked to me about programming. For whatever reason, I basically assumed that programming was just something I didn't know how to do and thus probably couldn't easily learn. I made one or two abortive efforts to learn using online tutorials about Python. I always gave up because there was no clear link between writing loops and doing arithmetic and the thing that I actually really cared about at that time: building virtual worlds.

High school did not go very well for me, from a typical perspective anyways. I did not like to attend my classes because they were mostly very boring and my attempts to engage with the material at a higher level were not usually appreciated, and the teachers who did appreciate it could not support it due to the nature of the institution. We had experienced some hardships as a family earlier in my life which put me in a bad relationship to school in general, and I usually felt extreme anxiety about having to go every day. I found that I could spend all day reading books, and usually tried to read during class to pass the time if the teacher didn't confiscate my book. Programming courses were offered but I don't think I was able to choose them as an elective for some reason and I didn't think very much on it.

In retrospect, my school system had no capability to identify people like me and try to help me get into the more challenging and interesting courses. I was not capable of realizing my predicament myself either. There was just not much to be done. I wanted to get away from school as fast as possible and I wasn't doing very well anyways so I dropped out. Afterwards I engaged with community college off and on, between finding jobs like data entry and dishwashing. Sometimes I did very well, sometimes I just stopped going to classes without really knowing why myself. I assiduously avoided math and science, although I had had extremely good experiences with earth science in high school, I thought I would one day become a history or philosophy profressor.

Initial efforts (2016-2020)

I eventually got a job in what might be called customer success or something like that today. At the time my title was just customer service representative for a big real estate website. We had maybe 8 of us in one part of a much larger office who handled all incoming calls to our support line. Generally it was real estate agents who had purchased web hosting or advertising with us and needed help fixing something or wanted to know why the leads they were getting weren't that good or things like that. My favorite calls were from regular people who just wanted help finding somewhere to live and thought that we could help. I would always stay on the phone with them and walk them through what I could, even taking them to other websites since ours wasn't always the best for up to date apartment rental listings. It reminds me of when I worked in a retail store prior, the best interaction I ever had at that job was an elderly man who wanted to buy bluetooth headphones for his television. He had been a USN officer and worked mostly with signals stuff, so he talked to me about how he presumed the headphones must work from his experience with that technology and I just happily listened.

What working in the call center did was expose me to working in an office for the first time. There was free coffee and bagels and team building exercises and opportunities for promotion. We also had a handful of the development team who worked on the site and other web apps in the office, and I could easily see how everyone let them be and they didn't have to answer any phones. My boss at the time told me that if I was interested in working in the development side that I could use my free time at work to study SQL and Javascript and such technologies and that it had been known to happen that people made these kinds of lateral moves if they got the skills needed. Looking back I could have just gone over to their part of the office and talked to them and let them know who I am and what I wanted to do and ask them for their advice but I was way too scared and shy.

But at this point, basic web programming had transformed from something I never thought about to something I could learn about. I started using online tutorials to learn about HTML, CSS, and SQL statements. I didn't really make much headway with Javascript as I kept putting it off for some time in the future when it would be more appropriate but I didn't know when or why that would occur. I was giving far more time to my study of Immanuel Kant's Critique of Pure Reason. I had decided that I would read all three of the great critiques and really know them well, inside and out. To prepare for this, I first studied Kant's lecture on logic, which would certainly have some small applicability to my later activities in programming but I couldn't say how significant it was. The largest benefit I gained from studying Kant was learning that I could take on an extremely difficult task for reasons that were entirely my own, one which would take quite a long time, and then see it through to some extent. Prior to this, I don't think I really engaged in any long term project that was entirely self-motivated which I didn't quit within a week or two.

I quit that job before any career moves were possible due to life circumstances and ended up moving away from my hometown. I sensed, perhaps rightly, that my ability to change and find a kind of life that I would be happy living was limited by remaining there in the place I had always been, being the kind of person I had always been, more or less. After moving, I lived in the spare room of a friend and read Moby Dick and a collection of other novels and philosophy books. I wandered around town a bit and drank lots of coffee and thought about life and what might be possible. I realized that I could go back to college again. In time, I was enrolled in the local community college which sat picturesque in the mountains.

I was determined to do better this time. I still spent a good deal of my time reading profusely in the local university library, books about the ancient world and Platonism and the middle ages and Anglo-Saxon society. But I also started studying math from where I had left off in high school. This meant starting basically at the beginning of high school algebra, learning to deal with fractions, how to factor equations. I made some good headway on my own, and when I took a placement test it was determined that I still needed one remedial course before I could take college math. It was a slight blow to my ego, but I took it in stride. Through some happenstance I ended up not being able to take many credits that semester, so I was just in the remedial math course and an online elective which I have forgotten. Otherwise, I worked a front desk job in the school's writing center and had a lot of time on my hands, so I decided to make an honest go of learning about computing.

I had come across the video AT&T Archives: The UNIX Operating System on YouTube around this time and it kindled a deep interest in me. What these guys were talking about and doing was freaking cool. Learning about how hardware works... writing an operating system... iterating on the tools and fundamental concepts of computing as a field?? My dreams were suddenly so much more lofty than moving across the office to get away from the phones. This was a tradition, it had a lineage of true craftsmen and scientists who had contributed to it and made it what it is today. I could become apart of something like that. I could actually practice and learn and become better and develop a craft that I could be proud of. This was the way I began to think.

I found Programming From the Ground Up by Jonathan Bartlett and used it to learn x86 assembly programming. I had played around a bit with C++ already so I understood some very basic concepts which I was now to learn more about, such as the stack, how function calls were executed, the Linux calling convention, what exactly is memory and how do we store and retrieve and interpret data. I got through some large section of the book and working through the practice exercises before setting it aside and starting on Bjarne Stroustrup's Programming: Principles and Practice Using C++. This book would prove to be as impactful on me for its ideological contents as it was for its practical advice. Thanks to my historical interest in the work of later Bell Labs, I was already biased towards lower level systems programming being more interesting and somehow better. Bjarne didn't do a great deal to disabuse me of that.

I simultaneously worked through the first half of Nand2Tetris after seeing it recommended on Reddit. I now knew more about programming than I ever had before, but it was deeply unsatisfying. Why do these text specifications we write do anything? What makes them do anything? How does this all run on our hardware? So I learned about how the transistor works and how you can implement simple logic gates and then build more complex functionality out of these simple gates and how this can be connected to stored data and how we derive simple computers in this way. This was the last true conceptual stumbling block now out of the way. I was no longer fundamentally in the dark about anything. I certainly didn't grasp anything in its true complex existence, but I now knew how I could pursue that knowledge and some of its basics. The idea that anything had to remain a black box to me was now done away with.

That was the spring semester of that year, and in the summer I was able to enroll in precalculus and trigonometry. It was a 5 credit course done in a very truncated timeline and I worked a great deal every day to keep pace. I didn't end up being the best in the class, but I believe it was a course that almost always had some attrition so the fact that I did generally well on top of passing made me feel pretty good. I used to believe that I would simply never be good at math but now I was learning that all it took was working hard and finding as much suplementary material as I could when I didn't understand something. Calculus I and II saw me actually improve and start to really enjoy math. I was asking my professor for real analysis textbook recommendations and started to study a bit of linear algebra on my own time.

During all of this I began taking the available programming courses at the community college. This consisted in writing Java programs to simulate card deck shuffling and dealing and writing up UML diagrams. None of it was very difficult but I was extremely happy to be able to be in a programming class at all and to no longer feel like there were barriers between me and being able to do something like that, so I had a good time and made the most of it. I started listening to interviews John Carmack had given about programming and 3D game technology and daydreaming about when I would know enough to be able to return to my lifelong fascination with 3D worlds and be able to create my own.

University (2020 - 2022)

I was accepted to transfer to the local university as a computer science major in the college of arts and sciences. Here I took another Java class which asked slightly more complicated things of us which were not very difficult, alongside classes on Homer and Greek Tragedy, Modern Japanese literature, Sir Thomas Malory's Le Morte D'Arthur, and I began to study Korean in earnest as I needed to satisfy a language requirement and had been interested in the Korean language and pop culture since the late 2000s when I made a Korean friend on the free games forum.

After the mandatory Java indoctrination was finally over I did Data Structure and Program Representation where we used C++ and a little x86 asm and I found that my prior experience served me quite well. When we were supposed to write and optimize a little assembly subroutine to pass an automated performance test I ended up reading a bunch of Agner Fog's site which did not really help me with assignment at all but introduced me to the fact that his site exists and that I could find such resources on my own efforts and go beyond a given scope of information.

The last CS courses I took in university were discrete math and mobile application development. I am glad to have the background in discrete math that I now do so that I don't feel inferior when such topics might come up, but it has also not directly informed very much of my programming practice. Mobile apps was conducted in React Native and was a crash course in both Javascript and React and mobile apps all at once. I was partnered with someone who wanted to do things that were too ambitious for those technologies and we ended up massively scaling back what we created by the end, and I found working with someone who insisted on unrealistic goals and then failed to work very hard to make them real to be somewhat taxing and demotivating.

The following semester I had enrolled in several CS classes, including a web application development course which mandated the formation of groups and a semester group project. Every day I felt severe dread about the course. I had discovered that I really didn't have much in common with my fellow CS majors. Most of them worked very hard on their homework and projects and grinding Leetcode but beyond that they gave no evidence at all of any actual interest in computing or its history. Their only reason for being in the industry was ultimately pecuniary. It is not that this made them bad people or anything, but what it did make them was people I couldn't talk to about computing. It left us with hardly any common ground on that front at all, and I didn't like the idea of having to now work with an entire group of them. Was this really why I did all of this? To be trained to build SaaS web apps with people who just wanted to make as much money as possible? Who didn't seem to actually have any curiosity or interest in anything?

So I dropped the major and switched into history. Immediately I felt a sense of relief. Upper level history courses assumed a great deal of autonomy and self motivation on the part of the student and I was able to push myself as far as I wanted to and recieved as much support as I asked for from the faculty. In addition to Korean I began studying Classical Chinese and in short order I was able to begin reading the Spring and Autumn Annals and Confucius in their original. I was far happier than I had ever been in a university CS course. My professors were finally actually accessible to me again and happy to talk to me about my interests and potential future. I started living in a language learning dorm and made friends with others studying Korean and other languages. I figured the old dream of history graduate school was back on the table. I would become fluent in Korean and go study premodern Korean history in South Korea, in Korean.

After graduation until now (2022 - 2025)

Many factors contributed to my decision not to follow through with this plan. One was that I did not receive a Fulbright Scholarship, which meant I needed to go forth on my own resources. Another was that while I had become very fluent in conversational Korean, I was unsure how well I would do in a university setting. I toyed with the idea of just going as an English teacher at first and continuing to work on my language skills. For the time being I got a job as a barista in town and continued hanging out with my school friends who hadn't yet graduated.

Over the course of several months I decided to do away with the graduate school notion entirely. The path was uncertain and financial realities were bleak and I now had new reasons to want to be financially stable soon. So I got a job at the library of my alma mater where I still work today. I reflected on the last several years and what I should be doing. I didn't regret the experience of studying history nor what I learned, but I hadn't lost interest in programming. I had merely found the reality of Computer Science programs to be distasteful. So returning to this was a natural idea.

It was clear to me that learning could no longer be of the elementary variety. Leanring needed to involve and result in projects, even of very small scope. I needed to figure out how to start making things on my own. And to do that I needed to decide what I wanted to make and what I could make. A CS major friend of mine had recommended that I go through Fullstack Open and practice DSA if I wanted to try to land a job, so I spent a week or so learning about building modern web apps using React and Node. I appreciate this experience for teaching me what goes into a modern web app and what they have to do to function, to some degree. I have used that basic knowledge many times since then, even if I have never since programmed a React frontend or a Node backend.

This inaugurated a struggle which I have periodically undergone since then, which is whether to work on projects with an eye towards finding a job or work on projects that I want to work on. What I have come to realize is that the typical advice given to learn Java or C# and their attendant frameworks to get access to the most jobs has a particular flaw in my case. The people who employ Java and C# programmers are not interested in applicants who don't have a CS degree or significant work experience, and in general most enterprise development seems to operate this way. I have yet to find a job posting from one of these places that doesn't appear to be generally hostile to my background and I have had little success getting any of them to offer me an interview.

Thankfully, the decision to make what I want to make has been what has largely prevailed. I decided to learn about network programming and quickly located Beej's Guide, which lead to a small telnet chat client and server program in C++, and a lot of knowledge about the network stack at a lower level. After concluding that, I decided that it was time to learn how to program a game without a commercial engine. No more putting it off. If I didn't start learning then I wouldn't ever learn. I took out Jason Gregory's Game Engine Architecture from the library and started reading. After I had read the first few chapters I realized that I was learning exactly what would need to learn in order to implement a game engine, but that the specifics I would need to figure out some other way.

Homebrew had been on my radar for awhile, so it seemed like an opportune time to give it a try. Writing a game on simpler hardware with an active community seemed like it would be a good introduction to the practice of game programming generally. So I joined the GBA Homebrew discord and set up the dev environmnt and Tonclib and got started. I was able to load tilemaps and a sprite to the screen and move the sprite around, so then it seemed that I needed to figure out how collision worked. I attempted to sketch out some basic notion of how I would be able to tell which tile was impassable and refuse to allow a move into that tile, but something about how I was going about it felt off to me and I couldn't figure out a simple and workable design. When I asked people in the homebrew community they gave me not very helpful answers about how I just needed to figure it out.

In my exploration of what game programming resources existed, Handmade Hero came up several times. I bookmarked the series but I was somewhat wary of the time commitment of needing to watch who knew how many hour-long streams before I got somewhere. But this roadblock convinced me to give it a shot. I was immediately addicted to the series and I coded along with Casey for each episode, maintaining my own codebase that I could play around with as we built new features. After around 30 episodes we had a background, a player character, sound, basic controls, and collision(!) all implemented. Having learned a lot of the basic things which I had set out to learn, I decided to strike off on my own and learn how to program using one of the 3D APIs.

Someone in the Graphics Programming discord very kindly had an open offer out to anyone wanting to learn graphics programming that they could DM him to engage in a correspondence course he had been developing where he gives you a task and will answer any questions you have while trying to complete it. I DMed him and the first task was get a cube on screen by any means necessary. I decided that Direct3D 11 would be a good beginning API, so I learned to set up the boilerplate and generated my first cubes. From there we proceeded feature by feature until I arrived at the current state of my 3D game engine.

Among the work and projects of the people I have met online, it is really not impressive in any way, not even worth consideration. Its extremely basic and could go a lot farther with some effort. I have barely scratched the surface of both graphics and engine programming. But it is something that I thought I could never do for a long time, and seeing it compile and run on my computer, and walking around in this little simple world which I was able to create, felt incredible. Now I knew that I could do it. But after working on it in my spare time for several months I wanted to take a break and find something else to make.

This lead me to Casey Muratori's latest effort, Computer Enhance's Performance Aware Programming series. I worked through part one where you read the Intel 8086 Manual and learn x86 instruction encoding in order to build first an 8086 instruction decoder and then an 8086 simulator which can run basic programs. On both this and the 3D engine project, I felt all of the Handmade Hero coding sessions had actually become internalized programming practice. I was going about exploratory programming and design in the same way I had watched Casey do it in HMH. None of my programs for CE ended up being anywhere as elegantly designed as Casey's examples were, but I was now confident that I could program whatever I wanted to program. When I needed to learn things I learned them and I now had the basic experience necessary to have confidence.

My only professional software experience, so far, occurred during this time. I managed to meet the manager of the software developers at my current job and asked him to let me work as an intern with their team. He seemed happy to let me come onboard and was very supportive. They were gearing up to do a big redesign project where we would all be working on greenfield stuff to reimplement this repository for scholarly articles in Go. For the first month or two I was mainly sitting in on design meetings with stakeholders and getting my development environment in order. Eventually I was tasked with writing a lambda in Go to retrieve metadata and send it along. I learned about AWS API Gateway and some other infra topics and was able to get it written satisfactorily within a day or so. I had my first commit to the project and all seemed well.

A week or two later, I was told that everyone was pulled off of the project by upper management to fast track a UI update that people were anxious to have completed on a shorter timeline. They were confident that they would be able to return to the redesign later on, so for now I would investigate a few features that I could work on alone while I waited for the project to reactivate. That day never came, and after many long periods of radio silence and people I was supposed to connect with not responding to me, I learned that upper management cancelled the project and decided to go with an off the shelf solution. This basically signaled the end of my internship. I had spent a good deal of time reading specs and thinking about ways to implement them while I waited, but in the end there wasn't a lot of will to continue to integrate me into their activities after getting that news and I read the writing on the wall.

Since then I have continued working on Computer Enhance. The next section of the course is about profiling using some syscalls and building a lightweight profiler you can easily integrate into your projects. I've also been learning about writing software 3D renderers while learning all of the 3D math which I either forgot or never learned. After watching Tsoding write a Wolfenstein 3D style renderer in Typescript, I coded along with him loosely, but changed the API when I disagreed with how he structured the renderer and also wrote better physics for the first person movement than he was using, all things I learned from HMH. You can see the result of my verson here. I've been reading Fabien Sanglard's Wolfenstein 3D Game Engine Black Book to get a better picture of what was going on in Wolfenstein and also the hardware of the time, which was the era just after the 8086 had been replaced in common use by the 386. I contributed a bug fix to the Freescape engine's software renderer in ScummVM, and one of the maintainers of that engine asked if I would help out with looking into removing the current software renderer and replacing it with a better one that they are scoping out. And that brings us to the present.

What I think of it all now

From the point of view of having the goal of finding employment as a programmer, a lot of what I have done could be seen as mistakes. I didn't start earlier, I didn't stick with the Python tutorials, I didn't remain in the CS program, I changed my mind too many times or was too ambivalent at crucial moments. But I don't really feel that way about things. I am happy that I have some accomplishments that are meaningful to me and that I have the ability and opportunity to continue programming and learning.

It is clear to me that engaging with software development solely to figure out how I can make money and get a job in the easiest possible manner is not sustainable for me. I spent months reading Kant, years learning Korean, and now even more years learning about programming and various domains within that field. I could only sustain the energy to move these projects forward at all because I am interested in them and enjoy them and am curious about them. I have gotten a lot of benefit out of seeing what people are working on, cool things created by solo developers that make me realize what I could be capable of in the future. I've also learned a lot from watching people like Casey, Jonathan Blow, and Tsoding stream their programming sessions. If you're on your own, it's the closest thing you can get to being an apprentice and learning how someone thinks and how they work on a very practical and personal level.

What I don't feel has benefited me is listening to advice from people online which is couched in any terms like "How to be a great engineer" and gives you simple rules to follow, like always read the documentation or don't ever think about performance or whatever. These are all clearly things that you decide depending on your situation and counter examples are easy to provide for nearly every rule on these lists. I have also not really benefited from watching people online talk about how you need to be programming at least 12 hours a day in order to be great, with the subtext here being that if you need to work for 8 hours at a non-sofware job to survive then you won't enter the industry.

It is not that I disagree with the idea that you get better at something by doing it. 10,000 hours is an old idea at this point and anyone who has been a true Khatzumoto disciple is well aware. But we all get there however we can. Sometimes you have more time to work on something, sometimes less. You respond to your conditions as you are able and move forward. Modern day SuccessWin cultists tend to throw around this idea of greatness and the sacrifice it requires which have absolutely no content. They don't exactly know what they mean when they deploy the word "great". They're hyping themselves up in order to keep going. I am tempted to suppose that they need this constant emotional stimulation because they have no actual interest in their day to day activities but have accepted that its what they have to do to be "great". So I have no interest in whatever this greatness is.

As Dan Luu rightly says, there's no reason to believe that my experience is able to be generalized into advice for anyone. Even if I was a senior engineer somewhere and people would really want to know how I got there, the truth is that everyone gets there by living their own life. You are dealing with your history as a person, the experiences you had as a child, how you are and what you like and what you hate, where you live, who you know, what you know, what you don't know, and where you're trying to go. There is no general advice for this. If I have wasted time doing anything, it was in trying to apply well meaning "successful" people's advice to situations they have never faced and know nothing about. In looking back on this narrative, it is clear that I have had goals for a long time which have had far more specificity and meaning to me than "I want to be a great programmer," or "I want to succeed and be rich." If I have ever been tempted to adopt these, it is because I temporarily lost faith in what I already knew I wanted to do, but I always cast them aside quickly as they are empty forms. So that's how I learned how to program.
